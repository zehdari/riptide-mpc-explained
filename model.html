Chapter 1

The Fossen model

An MPC is only as good as its prediction. riptide_mpc predicts with Thor Fossen's six-degree-of-freedom model of a rigid body moving through water, the same equations, in the same source file, that the team's simulator runs.

Everything the controller does downstream (the plan, the feedforward thrust, the linearization the solver works on) is a consequence of this one function: given where Talos is, how it is moving and how hard each thruster pushes, how fast does that motion change? This chapter builds it term by term, with Talos's identified numbers from config/models/talos.yaml.

The C++ is in marine_dynamics.cpp. Its header says it is copied from the simulator and must stay identical, and a unit test (test_dynamics_parity) compares the two whenever the simulator is installed. In simulation the controller's model and the plant are literally the same equations, so any tracking error you see in sim is the controller's fault, not the model's.

Frames and state

Two frames do most of the work. The world frame is the odometry frame: x and y on the pool's surface plane, z up, so depth is negative. The body frame is fixed to Talos and follows the ROS convention: x forward, y to port (left), z up. The physics is written about the centre of mass (COM), but odometry and every setpoint use base_link, a point a few centimetres away, so the model converts between the two everywhere.

The state is 13 numbers, the simulator's own layout:

x = \big[\; \underbrace{p}_{\text{COM position, world}} \;\big|\; \underbrace{q}_{\text{attitude } (w,x,y,z)} \;\big|\; \underbrace{v = (u, v, w)}_{\text{body velocity at COM}} \;\big|\; \underbrace{\omega = (p, q, r)}_{\text{body rates}} \;\big]

Fossen groups these as the pose \boldsymbol\eta = (p, q) and the body twist \boldsymbol\nu = (v, \omega). Attitude is a unit quaternion rather than roll-pitch-yaw angles so nothing is singular, including with the nose pointing straight down. The kinematics turn body motion into world motion:

\dot p = R(q)\, v, \qquad \dot q = \tfrac12\, q \otimes \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ \omega \end{bmatrix}

where R(q) is the rotation matrix of q and \otimes the quaternion product. After each integration step the quaternion is renormalized to unit length.

The equation of motion

The dynamics is Newton and Euler for a rigid body, plus the water. In Fossen's matrix form, with everything expressed in the body frame at the COM:

\mathbf M\,\dot{\boldsymbol\nu} + \mathbf C_{RB}(\boldsymbol\nu)\boldsymbol\nu + \mathbf C_A(\boldsymbol\nu_r)\boldsymbol\nu_r + \mathbf D(\boldsymbol\nu_r)\boldsymbol\nu_r + \mathbf g(\boldsymbol\eta) = \boldsymbol\tau_{\text{thrust}} + \boldsymbol\tau_{d}, \qquad \mathbf M = \mathbf M_{RB} + \mathbf M_A

νr = ν − νc is the velocity relative to the water. The pool has no current, so νr = ν here, but the code keeps the general form.

Read left to right: inertia (the body's and the water it drags along), Coriolis and centripetal effects of moving in a rotating frame, hydrodynamic drag, then gravity and buoyancy. On the right are the thrusters and \boldsymbol\tau_d, a disturbance wrench the estimator learns online to absorb model error (Chapter 7). Solving for the acceleration gives what the integrator needs:

\dot{\boldsymbol\nu} = \mathbf M^{-1}\big(\boldsymbol\tau_{\text{thrust}} + \boldsymbol\tau_d - \mathbf C_{RB}(\boldsymbol\nu)\boldsymbol\nu - \mathbf C_A(\boldsymbol\nu)\boldsymbol\nu - \mathbf D(\boldsymbol\nu)\boldsymbol\nu - \mathbf g(\boldsymbol\eta)\big)

Below, every term is evaluated by the browser's copy of the model at whatever motion you dial in. Watch which terms matter at Talos's speeds.

Mass and added mass

The rigid-body part is plain mass and inertia about the COM: 34.3 kg and the CAD inertia tensor.

\mathbf M_{RB} = \begin{bmatrix} m I_3 & 0 \\ 0 & I_g \end{bmatrix}, \qquad m = 34.307\ \text{kg}

Accelerating a body in water also accelerates the water around it, and that water's momentum has to come from somewhere. Fossen models it as added mass \mathbf M_A, a symmetric matrix the vehicle carries around as if it were its own. For a frame robot with flat panels it is large: pushing forward, Talos behaves as if it weighed 158 kg, more than four times its own mass.

The model checks \mathbf M is symmetric positive definite and factors it once with a Cholesky decomposition, so evaluating \mathbf M^{-1} each step is a stored 6×6 product.

Coriolis and centripetal terms

Because \boldsymbol\nu is measured in a rotating frame, momentum that is constant in the world still changes in body coordinates. riptide_mpc uses the momentum form from Fossen's MSS toolbox (m2c), which works for any symmetric mass matrix including off-diagonal added mass. With the momentum h = \mathbf M \boldsymbol\nu = (h_1, h_2):

\mathbf C(\mathbf M, \boldsymbol\nu) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -S(h_1) \\ -S(h_1) & -S(h_2) \end{bmatrix}, \qquad S(a)\,b = a \times b

It is applied twice: to \mathbf M_{RB} with the absolute velocity and to \mathbf M_A with the velocity relative to the water. One consequence is worth knowing: the added-mass part produces a yaw moment when Talos moves forward and sideways at once, the Munk moment, N = (A_{vv} - A_{uu})\,u\,v \approx -44\,uv N·m. At 0.5 m/s forward with a 0.1 m/s slip that is 2.2 N·m, as much as the yaw drag at 0.5 rad/s. A controller without the model would see it as an unexplained yaw disturbance; the MPC predicts it and counters it before it turns the robot.

Damping

Drag is modelled per axis as linear plus quadratic in the relative velocity. Both matrices are diagonal for Talos:

\mathbf D(\boldsymbol\nu)\boldsymbol\nu = \mathbf D_l\, \boldsymbol\nu + \mathbf D_q\, |\boldsymbol\nu| \circ \boldsymbol\nu

The quadratic term dominates above walking pace. By frontal area you would expect surge to have the most drag: moving forward pushes Talos's biggest face (about 0.83 × 0.55 m) through the water, against roughly 0.35 × 0.83 m for heave and 0.35 × 0.55 m for sway. The identified quadratic terms agree on surge over sway (224 against 154), though sway's large linear term brings the two level near 0.5 m/s. Heave looks highest on the chart, but its drag is the shakiest number here. It comes from the oldest session (2026-10-03, flown while an ESC firmware bug needed a 140 rpm offset); every session since has rejected the heave fit, because the heave lanes are too short to reach a steady speed. Treat that curve with suspicion.

Drag also explains a limit you will meet later: the thrusters can deliver at most 73 N in total (the power budget), and surge drag reaches 73 N at 0.54 m/s. The controller's speed limit of 0.6 m/s is above what the model can sustain, so on a long straight move the reference governor (Chapter 4) has to slow the reference down.

The code applies drag at an optional offset damping centre and maps it back to the COM with the same lever arm on the way in (velocity) and out (force), which keeps the term dissipative. Talos's centre is at the COM.

Restoring forces: gravity and buoyancy

Weight acts at the COM; buoyancy acts at the centre of buoyancy (COB) and points straight up in the world. Talos displaces 34.5 litres:

W = mg = 336.4\ \text{N}, \qquad B = \rho g \nabla = 338.1\ \text{N}

so it is 1.6 N positively buoyant: lose power and it floats up, slowly. In the body frame, with r_b the COB relative to the COM:

\mathbf g(\boldsymbol\eta) = -\begin{bmatrix} R(q)^\top \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\B - W\end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] r_b \times R(q)^\top \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\B\end{bmatrix} \end{bmatrix}

The COB sits 22.5 mm above the COM, so any tilt produces a torque back toward level, like a pendulum. That stiffness, about B\,\overline{BG} \approx 7.6 N·m per radian, is what lets the MPC weight roll and pitch heavily without fighting the vehicle: the hull wants to be level anyway.

Near the surface the model goes one step further: buoyancy is scaled by how much of an ellipsoid around the COB is still under water, and the wet centre moves with it. The thrusters get the same treatment, a propeller disc half out of the water gives roughly half its thrust. At the depths Talos works these factors are exactly one; they matter for surfacing, where the simulator and controller agree on the physics.

Integrating it

The MPC never uses a continuous-time model; it uses this one step, run over and over. Within one step of length h the thrusters' realized force evolves for h/2, the body is advanced with classic fourth-order Runge–Kutta holding that force, then the thrusters evolve for the other h/2:

// fossen_model.cpp, FossenModel::step: one physics_simulator step
evolveActuators(force, target, h / 2);
rk4(x, h, [&](const State13d &s) { return derivative(s, force); });
evolveActuators(force, target, h / 2);

The prediction rollout uses h = 2 ms, the simulator's own step, so in simulation it reproduces the plant to rounding. The Jacobians use a coarser 25 ms model (two steps per 50 ms stage), which is accurate enough for derivatives and twelve times cheaper. Chapter 6 explains where each is used.

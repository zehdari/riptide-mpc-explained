Chapter 6

Feedback and model error

A prediction is only useful if it starts from the right place and the model behind it is honest. The estimator handles the first; a learned disturbance wrench handles the second, and makes the controller offset-free.

Why the MPC has its own estimator

Talos already runs an EKF (robot_localization) that fuses the same sensors. The MPC uses it only as an anchor, because for control it is late. Bench and pool tests on 2026-10-27 measured its velocity 0.2–0.35 s behind the DVL, and its roll and pitch rates about 0.45 s behind the IMU. Half a second of lag in the feedback of a controller planning 1.5 s ahead is a third of its horizon. (Both were tuning problems: the IMU reported its gyro about 10,000× noisier than it is, and the EKF's velocity process noise was tiny. Fixing them the same day brought the rates to about 45 ms on the bench and the velocity to about 0.1 s in pool-bag replays. The MPC still builds its own state, which is on time regardless.)

So state_estimator.cpp builds the MPC's state directly from raw sensors (state_source: sensors in mpc.yaml). Between measurements it propagates the same Fossen model, driven by a replica of the thrusters fed every command the MPC publishes. Each measurement then corrects the channel it observes.

The corrections are complementary-filter style: fast sensors are trusted outright, slow or drifting references are blended in with a time constant \tau, using a gain that accounts for the time since the last sample,

x \leftarrow x + k\,(z - h(x)), \qquad k = 1 - e^{-\Delta t / \tau}

with \Delta t capped at \tau so a dropout never makes one sample jump the state. A few details carry most of the value:

  • Body rates come straight from the gyros (gain 1), with the fibre-optic gyro owning the yaw axis when it is fresh. No filter lag at all on the fastest channel.
  • Roll and pitch from gravity, slowly. The IMU's orientation is used only for its "up" direction, blended in with a 1 s time constant by rotating about \hat z_{\text{meas}} \times \hat z_{\text{est}}, which corrects tilt without touching heading. The gyro carries the fast motion.
  • The DVL measures its own point. Its velocity is v + \omega \times r_{\text{dvl}}, so the estimator compares against that, using the fresh gyro rate. Otherwise every rotation would read as translation. DVL messages arrive about 125 ms after the measurement, so the estimator compares them with its own velocity at the measurement's timestamp (from a one-second history) and applies the correction now.
  • The EKF anchors x, y and heading with 2 s time constants (nothing else observes them), and stands in, faster, for any sensor that goes stale. If the two disagree by more than 1 m or 0.5 rad, the estimator re-seeds from the EKF.

Learning what the model is missing

No identified model is exact. Talos's buoyancy changes with a new payload; the COB moves when a battery does; a thruster gets weaker. Any of these leaves a constant wrench the model does not know about, and a controller that predicts without it holds position slightly off: the classic steady-state offset of MPC with model error.

The estimator sees that error directly. After propagating the model between two DVL samples, the velocity it predicted differs from the one measured by some innovation e_v. A wrench the model is missing would cause exactly \Delta v = \mathbf M^{-1}\tau_{\text{miss}}\,\Delta t, so the innovation is evidence of a missing wrench \mathbf M e_v / \Delta t. riptide_mpc moves its estimate \hat d toward it with a time constant:

\hat d_F \leftarrow \hat d_F + \gamma\, \frac{R(q)\, \mathbf M_{v}\, e_v}{\tau_F}, \qquad \hat d_\tau \leftarrow \hat d_\tau + \gamma\, \frac{\mathbf M_{\omega}\, e_\omega}{\tau_\tau}, \qquad \tau_F = \tau_\tau = 3\ \text{s}

with \mathbf M_v, \mathbf M_\omega the translational and rotational blocks of the mass matrix. The force is kept in the world frame (buoyancy error points up whatever the attitude), the torque in the body frame (a COB offset rotates with the hull). Both are clamped, to 20 N and 5 N·m.

Then the key step: \hat d is added to the model the MPC predicts with, in every stage, as the \boldsymbol\tau_d of Chapter 1. It also enters the feedforward, through the model's unforced acceleration. This is offset-free MPC (Pannocchia and Rawlings, 2003): at steady state the innovations are zero only when the model plus \hat d reproduces the plant, and the controller, predicting with that, cancels the disturbance exactly. It acts like integral action, but through the model, so it does not wind up and does not fight the plan.

One refinement keeps it honest. Speed-dependent error, mostly drag, would be learned while moving and linger after stopping, so learning is slowed with speed:

\gamma = \frac{1}{1 + (|v| / 0.1\ \text{m/s})^2} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + (|\omega| / 0.2\ \text{rad/s})^2}

The disturbance estimate is meant to capture statics; the feedback loop handles the rest.

From the pool

The learned disturbance doubles as a model check. In a well-identified hold it should sit near zero, so a steady value says the statics are off. On 2026-10-26 an untethered run flew with a model fitted while tethered: the tether's pull had been absorbed into the fitted buoyancy and COB. The MPC's disturbance settled at +2.7 N·m pitch, +0.7 N·m roll and +1.3 N heave, pointing straight at the contaminated terms. Lesson: never identify statics with a tether on.

Has it arrived?

Autonomy needs to know when a move is done. The node publishes a settle trust per axis on controller/scale/trust, a number in [0, 1] that the behaviour tree's WaitForController waits on (typically above 0.8–0.99). It is reset to 0 whenever the setpoint moves, and grows only while the profile has reached the setpoint and the vehicle is within 2 cm, 2°, 3 cm/s and 0.03 rad/s of it:

\text{trust} \leftarrow \begin{cases} 1 - e^{-\Delta t/0.25}\,(1 - \text{trust}) & \text{settled} \\ \text{trust}\cdot e^{-\Delta t / 1.0} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}

so it passes 0.95 about a second after arrival, and one noisy sample barely dents it. A FollowPath goal succeeds when trust reaches 0.8 on the last point, and fails if the reference makes less than 5 cm of progress in 10 s.

The node around it

mpc_controller_node.cpp turns all of this into a ROS 2 controller. A 20 Hz timer runs the tick: advance the thruster replica and the reference by the elapsed time, update the estimate, hand the disturbance to the controller, compute, publish. If the vehicle is killed, motion is disabled or odometry is stale, it publishes zero thrust and clears the warm start instead, so it restarts cleanly.

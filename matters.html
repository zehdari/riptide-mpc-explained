Chapter 7

Which numbers matter

A model has dozens of numbers, and they don't all matter at the same time. Holding still depends on a handful you measure in the lab; moving depends on the ones only the pool can tell you.

Where each number comes from

Before Talos goes in the water for identification, most of its model already exists. The pool session only fits what can't be measured on land, and it fits those around the numbers you give it, so the land numbers have to be right first.

The ordering matters because identification is a fit of the difference between what the trusted numbers predict and what the robot does. The statics fit, for example, finds the net buoyancy and the buoyancy moment that balance the thrust at each hold, given the mass and COM you supplied. Change the mass or move the COM afterwards (a new claw, a battery moved) and the fitted volume and COB are now paired with the wrong body; the model file has to be re-expressed for the new one, which is what the comments at the top of talos.yaml record every time it happened.

Why holding depends on the lab numbers

Write the equation of motion from Chapter 1 for a vehicle holding still. With \boldsymbol\nu = 0 and \dot{\boldsymbol\nu} = 0, every term with a velocity or an acceleration in it vanishes: drag, added mass, Coriolis, all of it. What is left is a balance between thrust and the restoring wrench:

\mathbf T f_{\text{hold}} = \mathbf g(\boldsymbol\eta) - \hat{\boldsymbol\tau}_d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{heave: } W - B, \qquad \text{trim: } r_b \times B

So at a hold, the model's whole job is to know two things: how much Talos weighs against how much it displaces, and where those two forces act relative to each other. Both are differences of large numbers. Weight and buoyancy are each about 337 N, and Talos's net buoyancy is their difference, 1.6 N. A 1% error in either (0.34 kg on the scale, or 0.35 litres of CAD volume) is 3.4 N, twice the true net buoyancy. Likewise the trim torque is 338 N times the horizontal offset between COB and COM: every 5 mm of COM error is 1.7 N·m of pitch the model doesn't know about.

The thrust curve matters much less here, because it multiplies the hold thrust, and the hold thrust is small: a few newtons of trim. A 10% curve error at a hold is a fraction of a newton.

Why moving depends on the pool numbers

While moving, the feedforward of Chapter 4 asks for \boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf M\dot{\boldsymbol\nu} + \mathbf D(\boldsymbol\nu)\boldsymbol\nu + \dots: inertia times acceleration and drag times speed, tens of newtons, all through the thrust curve. Now added mass, drag and the curve decide whether the plan is right, and an error in them is an error in every newton the controller commands. These are also exactly the errors the disturbance estimate can't fix, because its learning is gated off with speed (Chapter 6): it is built to soak up statics, not drag.

The numbers below are from the browser port: the simulated Talos differs from the controller's model in one parameter, by an error typical of how that parameter is obtained. The hold runs have disturbance learning off, to show what the error does on its own; the move runs have it on, and move 1.5 m forward after a 20 s hold.

Three things stand out:

  • Mass and volume trade one for one. Their hold errors are identical, because at a hold only W - B matters. That's also why identification fits the volume against a mass you trust: the pool can't tell them apart.
  • COM and COB trade the same way, with opposite signs. A vertical COB error does nothing at a level hold (the righting torque only appears when tilted), which is why the statics session tilts Talos 25°.
  • The surge added mass is the biggest move error here. With the 32 kg that talos.yaml carried, tracking error during a surge more than triples. Drag and the thrust curve are next. None of them shows up in a hold at all, so a robot can hold beautifully and still move badly.

In simulation, Talos barely pitches during surge moves even with these errors. In the pool it pitches 5–10° on surge starts, so part of the real robot's behaviour is still outside the model.

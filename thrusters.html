Chapter 2

Thrusters and allocation

Eight thrusters, six ways to move. How thrust becomes a wrench, which thrust patterns do nothing at all, and why a thruster that answers 30 ms late has to be part of the model.

From eight forces to one wrench

Each thruster i sits at a position r_i relative to the COM and pushes along a unit axis d_i. A force f_i along that axis is a force f_i d_i plus a moment r_i \times f_i d_i about the COM. Stack the columns and the whole propulsion system is one linear map, the thruster matrix:

\boldsymbol\tau_{\text{thrust}} = \mathbf T f, \qquad \mathbf T = \begin{bmatrix} d_1 & d_2 & \cdots & d_8 \\ r_1 \times d_1 & r_2 \times d_2 & \cdots & r_8 \times d_8 \end{bmatrix} \in \R^{6\times 8}

The poses come from the vehicle YAML (riptide_descriptions/config/talos.yaml), the same file the simulator and URDF use. FossenModel::fromNodes subtracts the COM from each position and rotates the x axis by each thruster's roll-pitch-yaw to get d_i. On Talos, four thrusters at the corners point straight forward, and four are angled at 45° so each pushes sideways and vertically at once.

Read the graph from the axes up. Surge is only the four forward thrusters; sway and heave are only the four angled ones, mixed with opposite signs; yaw is the forward thrusters pushing differentially port against starboard. No axis belongs to one thruster, and no thruster serves one axis. That coupling is the first reason to let an optimizer pick the thrusts.

Thrust that does nothing

\mathbf T is 6×8 with rank 6, so it has a two-dimensional null space: thrust patterns with \mathbf T f = 0. Spin the thrusters in one of these patterns and Talos does not move at all. Any wrench can be produced by infinitely many thrust vectors, differing by null-space components.

The null space is useful. pool_identify adds a null-space bias to the commands while it identifies the thrusters, so thrusters can run at known levels while the MPC still holds the vehicle still and tracking costs nothing. hardware.spin_bias exists for the same reason: the team's ESCs take around 0.8 s to start a motor from rest, so keeping every thruster spinning gently in a zero-wrench pattern avoids that dead time.

Allocation, and why the MPC skips it

A classical controller computes a desired wrench and then allocates it to thrusters, usually with the minimum-norm solution f = \mathbf T^{+}\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf T^\top(\mathbf T\mathbf T^\top)^{-1}\boldsymbol\tau. riptide_mpc uses exactly this inside its feedforward (MpcController::allocate, via a complete orthogonal decomposition), but not for control. The MPC never forms a desired wrench. Its decision variables are the eight thruster commands themselves, over the whole horizon, with the ±24 N limits as box constraints of the QP. Saturation is handled where it belongs, by the optimizer, which trades position error against attitude error knowing exactly what the thrusters can do. It is the same reason the solver can lean on the null space when a thruster is pinned.

Two more limits sit outside the QP. The command is scaled down as a whole if the sum of absolute forces exceeds the 73 N power budget (limitTotalThrust), which keeps the wrench's direction. And identification can pin individual thrusters to fixed values; the MPC then sets that stage's bounds equal and optimizes the rest.

Thrust is late

A thruster does not produce the force it was asked for the instant it is asked. thruster_dynamics.cpp models three effects, per thruster:

  • A transport delay of 30 ms: every command waits in a queue before the motor sees it (CAN, the ESC, the motor controller).
  • A first-order lag toward the commanded force, with time constant 80 ms when spinning up and 60 ms when spinning down: f \leftarrow f + (f_{\text{target}} - f)(1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau}).
  • A slew limit of 300 N/s and the ±24 N saturation.

Because the lag is a state, the MPC carries it in its own state vector: the 13 body states plus the 8 realized thruster forces. That is why the controller's linearized state has dimension 20 (12 on the tangent space plus 8), and why it can plan a command that leads the force it actually wants. It also keeps a replica of the delay queue (MpcController::actuator_), fed every published command, so it always knows what is still in flight. Chapter 4 shows how it uses that to plan through the delay.

From the pool

The model also has an optional slow time constant for a motor that is reversing or starting from rest (sensorless ESCs take 0.5–0.8 s there). It is off by default, because the simulator's copy has no such term and the two must stay identical. Slow starts were once blamed for identification's large surge inertia; a lag check later showed that inertia is real (Chapter 1).

From force to RPM

The ESCs take RPM, not newtons. Talos uses Blue Robotics T200 thrusters, and its force-to-RPM curve comes straight from Blue Robotics' published T200 performance data. The model file stores that data as a propeller law per direction, |F| = K_T\,\rho D^4 (n/60)^2, with a thrust coefficient that grows slightly with speed, K_T = a + b\,n, fitted to the published curve. The node inverts it with a few Newton iterations (HardwareConfig::forceToRpm). Reverse thrust is weaker: the propeller is shaped for forward.
